Cherian, Gibbs & Candès · NeurIPS 2024 · arXiv 2406.09714

Three upgrades to conformal claim filtering

Conformal factuality filters an LLM answer. It drops the claims a scorer trusts least, so that what remains has no false claim with probability \(1-\alpha\). This paper keeps that filter and improves three things about it. The threshold depends on the prompt, so the guarantee holds within groups of prompts. The level depends on the prompt, so the answer stays useful and carries a calibrated probability. The scorer is trained through the procedure, so more claims survive. Toy numbers and schematic figures on this page are illustrations; the results are the paper's.

Starting point · Mohri & Hashimoto

One filter, one threshold

Split the answer into claims, score each claim, and keep the ones at or above a threshold. The marks show where each of the paper's three changes acts.

How the one threshold is set

Each labelled calibration answer is reduced to one number \(S_i\): the score of its highest-scoring false claim. Any threshold above \(S_i\) leaves that answer error-free. The threshold is a conformal quantile of these numbers.

\[ \hat\tau=S_{(k)},\quad k=\lceil(1-\alpha)(n+1)\rceil \qquad\text{keep } C_j \text{ if } p(C_j)\ge\hat\tau \]
What this leaves open
  • The guarantee is an average over prompts. It can be too lax on hard prompts and too strict on easy ones.
  • At a 90% level with a weak scorer, almost every claim is removed.
  • The scorer is fixed. Nothing tunes it for the filter it feeds.
Upgrade 1 · Sec. 3.1 · Thm. 3.1

The threshold depends on the prompt

Replace the single quantile with a quantile regression of \(S_i\) on features of the prompt.

Choose features \(\Phi(X)\) of the prompt and answer, for example indicators of topic groups. Fit a quantile regression of the calibration scores on those features. The threshold for a new prompt is the fitted quantile at its features.

\[ \hat\tau(X)=\Phi(X)^\top\hat\beta,\qquad \hat\beta=\arg\min_{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_\alpha\big(S_i-\Phi(X_i)^\top\beta\big) \]

\(\ell_\alpha\) is the pinball loss, whose minimiser is the \(1-\alpha\) quantile. The paper's fit also includes the test point with an imputed score (Eq. 7), which is what makes the guarantee hold in finite samples. With a constant feature this is the starting point again.

Guaranteed The filtered answer is error-free with probability \(1-\alpha\) within every group encoded in the features, and no longer only on average. The same construction covers looser targets such as "at most 3 false claims".

Toy, \(\alpha=0.2\), six answers per group. The scorer is more reliable on popular topics, so their false claims score lower. One pooled threshold removes more claims than popular topics need, and too few for rare topics. The paper finds this pattern on Wikipedia biographies grouped by page views (Fig. 6).

Upgrade 2 · Sec. 3.2 · Thm. 3.2

The level depends on the prompt

Fix what must survive, then issue the strongest guarantee each prompt allows.

A fixed 90% level is the wrong promise for a hard prompt, because meeting it means deleting the answer. The paper turns the question around.

  • Pick a usefulness target, such as keeping at least 70% of the claims.
  • On separate data, learn a function \(\alpha(X)\) that gives each prompt the smallest \(\alpha\) expected to meet the target.
  • Calibrate as in upgrade 1, with each point using its own level: \(\ell_\alpha\) becomes \(\ell_{\alpha(X_i)}\).
  • Report \(1-\alpha(X)\) with the filtered answer.
Guaranteed The issued level is calibrated: among answers issued 70%, about 70% are error-free. This needs \(\alpha(\cdot)\) to be among the regression features.
Not guaranteed That the target is met, or that the level is high. A weak scorer gets an honest low number.

Upgrade 3 · Sec. 3.3 · Conditional boosting

The scorer is trained through the filter

Rehearse the calibration on training data, and move the scorer's weights so that more claims clear the threshold.

The scorer \(p_\theta\) has weights \(\theta\). Each step splits the training data at random, fits the threshold on one half, and counts the claims kept on the other.

\[ \max_\theta\ \sum_{i\,\in\,\text{held-out}}\ \sum_{j}\ \mathbf 1\big\{\,p_\theta(C_{ij})\ge\hat\tau_i(\theta)\,\big\} \]

Two things block the gradient, and each has a fix.

  • The indicator is a step. Replace it with a sigmoid, as ConfTr does.
  • The threshold depends on \(\theta\). Quantile regression is a linear program. Its fitted line passes exactly through \(d\) of the calibration points, called the basis \(B\), where \(d\) is the number of features. So the threshold is a linear function of those \(d\) scores and can be differentiated exactly.
\[ \hat\tau_i(\theta)=\Phi(X_i)^\top\,\Phi_B^{-1}\,S_B(\theta) \]
At test time Freeze \(\theta\), then run upgrades 1 and 2 as usual on fresh calibration data. Nothing is smoothed there, so the guarantees hold for the trained scorer.
Two details that matter The objective counts every kept claim, true or false. Labels enter only through the calibration scores \(S_i\), which set the threshold. What is trained is small: three or four weights that mix existing claim scores, such as agreement across resampled answers and the model's self-reported confidence.
Results · Sec. 1.1, 4.2

What it buys

Medical long-form question answering (MedLFQA), with zero false claims as the target.

Mean share of claims kept at a 90% level

  • Training the scorer raises the mean share of claims kept from 24% to 39%.
  • Adapting the level, with a target of 70% of claims kept, meets the target on most examples, and the share of error-free answers tracks the issued level.
  • The limit: the filter is only as good as its scores. Training reweights existing scores and cannot add signal they lack.

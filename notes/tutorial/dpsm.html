Shi, Shahrokhi, Jia, Chen, Doppa & Yan · ICML 2025 · Direct Prediction Set Minimization via Bilevel Conformal Classifier Training

How DPSM learns the conformal quantile instead of reading it off each mini-batch

Conformal training (the ConfTr page) trains a classifier for small conformal prediction sets by computing a conformal threshold on every mini-batch. That threshold is a noisy stand-in for the true quantile of the scores. This paper proves that, under two assumptions, the error this causes in the training objective is at least of order \(1/s\), for batch size \(s\). Direct Prediction Set Minimization (DPSM) instead makes the quantile a single learned number \(q\), fits it by quantile regression while the classifier trains, and so turns conformal training into a bilevel problem whose error bound is \(\tilde O(1/\sqrt n)\) in the number \(n\) of training examples, whatever the batch size. After training, sets come from ordinary split conformal prediction, as before. Figures marked toy use simulated numbers; numbers from the paper name the table, figure or section they come from.

Quantile-based conformal training: a soft set size at the true quantile (toy)

\(\tau_{\rm Sigmoid}\) (toy)

Conformal training trades accuracy against "conformal alignment" on the training set (Eq. 3). Following ConfTr, the alignment loss is the expected soft set size, with the indicator replaced by a sigmoid:

\[ \min_f\ \mathcal L_{\rm cls}(f)+\lambda\cdot\mathcal L_c(f)\qquad(3) \]
\[ \begin{aligned}\mathcal L_c(f)&=\ell(f,Q_f),\\ \ell(f,q)&\triangleq\mathbb E_X\Big[\textstyle\sum_{y\in\mathcal Y}\tilde\one\big[S_f(X,y)\le q\big]\Big]\end{aligned}\qquad(4) \]
\[ \tilde\one[S\le q]=\frac{1}{1+\exp\!\big(-(q-S)/\tau_{\rm Sigmoid}\big)} \]

\(\mathcal L_{\rm cls}\) is e.g. cross-entropy and \(\lambda\) a weight. \(\ell(f,q)\) accepts any threshold \(q\); the loss we want, \(\mathcal L_c(f)\), plugs in the true quantile \(Q_f\). Part 2 is about why that is hard.

The DPSM paper writes ConfTr's loss in this form (Eq. 14), without the log and the \(\kappa\) of the original paper. It does not report the \(\tau_{\rm Sigmoid}\) it uses.

Part 2 · The difficulty · Sec. 3

The true quantile \(Q_f\) moves every time \(f\) does

The loss needs \(Q_f\) at every update, and \(Q_f\) is not a quantity training can read off cheaply.

In the paper's words, "\(Q_f\) is an implicit function of \(f\)". Two consequences:

  • It moves. Every update of \(f\) changes every score, so it changes \(Q_f\) too.
  • It is expensive to recompute. The empirical quantile \(\widehat Q_f\) on \(\mathcal D_{\rm tr}\) needs all \(n\) training scores under the new \(f\), sorted: \(O(n\log n)\) per update.

There are two ways out: estimate \(Q_f\) from the current mini-batch (ConfTr and CUT, Part 3) or learn it alongside \(f\) (DPSM, Parts 4–6).

Toy: histograms of \(n=1{,}999\) training scores \(S_f(X_i,Y_i)\) as a toy classifier improves; the dark tail right of \(Q_f\) holds about \(10\%\) of them (\(\alpha=0.1\)). The same toy run returns in Parts 3 and 5. Training is mimicked by moving every score down on a fixed schedule.

Part 3 · Prior methods · Sec. 3 · Eq. 5 · Prop. 3.4 · Thm. 3.5 · App. A–B

ConfTr and CUT: plug in the quantile of the current mini-batch

The paper calls this stochastic approximation (SA). It is cheap, but the batch quantile is a random draw, and its noise does not average out of the objective.

Eq. 5

The loss SA training actually minimises

At each iteration, take the empirical quantile \(\hat q_f\) of a mini-batch \(\mathcal B\) of \(s\) training examples and use it as the threshold. Since \(\hat q_f\) depends on which batch was drawn, the paper treats it as a random variable, \(\hat q_f\sim\mathcal Q_f\). The "in-effect" conformal loss is then

\[ \widehat{\mathcal L}^{\rm SA}_c(f)=\mathbb E_{\hat q_f\sim\mathcal Q_f}\big[\hat\ell(f,\hat q_f)\big],\qquad \hat\ell(f,q)\triangleq\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\sum_{y\in\mathcal Y}\tilde\one\big[S_f(X_i,y)\le q\big]\Big]\qquad(5) \]

ConfTr (Stutz et al., 2021), Eq. 14

\[ \widehat{\mathcal L}^{\rm SA}_{c,\rm CTr}(f)=\mathbb E_{\hat q_f\sim\mathcal Q_f}\Big[\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{y\in\mathcal Y}\tilde\one\big[S_f(X_i,y)\le\hat q_f\big]\Big] \]
Soft set size at the batch quantile, which ConfTr makes differentiable with a smooth sort (ConfTr page).

CUT (Einbinder et al., 2022), Eq. 15

\[ \widehat{\mathcal L}^{\rm SA}_{c,\rm CUT}(f)=\mathbb E_{\hat q_f\sim\mathcal Q_f}\Big[\sup_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\big|(1-\alpha)-\hat q_f(\alpha)\big|\Big] \]
Penalises how far the batch's score quantiles \(\hat q_f(\alpha)\) are from those of a uniform distribution, aiming at conditional coverage. Same plug-in of batch quantiles.

Einbinder et al. (2022) had shown \(\big|\widehat{\mathcal L}^{\rm SA}_c(f)-\mathcal L_c(f)\big|\le O(1/\sqrt s)\). The DPSM paper adds a lower bound (Theorem 3.5, below).

1Which training score becomes \(\hat q_f\)

The batch quantile is one of the \(n\) training scores, \(S_{(j)}\) for a random \(j\). Proposition 3.4 gives the probability of event \(Z(j)\), "\(S_{(j)}\) is selected as \(\hat q_f\)":

\[ \mathbb P\big(Z(j)\big)=\frac{\binom{j}{\lceil(1-\alpha)(s+1)\rceil}\binom{n-j-1}{s-\lceil(1-\alpha)(s+1)\rceil-1}}{\binom{n}{s}} \]

For large \(n\), as a function of \(j/n\) this has the shape of a Beta density with parameters \(\lceil(1-\alpha)(s+1)\rceil+1\) and \(s-\lceil(1-\alpha)(s+1)\rceil\): it peaks near \(1-\alpha\) and narrows like \(1/\sqrt s\).

The figure counts directly: \(S_{(j)}\) is the batch's \(k\)-th smallest score, \(k=\lceil(1-\alpha)(s+1)\rceil\), with probability \(\binom{j-1}{k-1}\binom{n-j}{s-k}/\binom{n}{s}\). The paper's formula is this with \(j\) and \(k\) both shifted by one; the shapes agree.

3The error shrinks with \(s\), and only with \(s\)

\[ \Omega(1/s)\ \le\ \big|\widehat{\mathcal L}^{\rm SA}_c(f)-\mathcal L_c(f)\big|\ \le\ \tilde O(1/\sqrt s) \]

Theorem 3.5, with probability at least \(1-\delta\), under Assumptions 3.1 and 3.2 (Part 7), when \((1-\alpha)(s+1)\) is not an integer, the rounding gap \(\lceil(1-\alpha)(s+1)\rceil-(1-\alpha)(s+1)\) is at least \(\Omega(1/s)\), and \(s\le\sqrt n\). \(\tilde O\) hides log factors.

The proof (App. B) shows where each bound comes from:

  • Upper, order \(1/\sqrt s\): the spread of \(\hat q_f\) (Lemma B.5, by Chebyshev's inequality).
  • Lower, order \(1/s\): two effects that averaging over batches cannot remove. The mean of \(\hat q_f\) sits off \(Q_f\) because the batch rank is rounded up (Lemma B.4). And because the soft size is locally strongly concave in \(q\) (Assumption 3.2), averaging it over a random \(\hat q_f\) differs from its value at the mean by about the variance of \(\hat q_f\) (Lemma B.3).

Toy, computed exactly from the distribution above. The offset jumps around because the rounding gap \(\lceil 0.9(s+1)\rceil-0.9(s+1)\) changes with \(s\); with \(s=16\) the rank is \(16=s\), so \(\hat q_f\) is the batch maximum. Dotted guides have slopes \(-\tfrac12\) and \(-1\).

What the batch quantile does well It is cheap: sort \(s\) scores, not \(n\). It is computed with the current \(f\), so it follows \(f\) immediately. In ConfTr it is also differentiable.
What it costs An error band from \(\Omega(1/s)\) to \(\tilde O(1/\sqrt s)\) in the conformal loss, set by the batch size. In the common setting \(s\le\sqrt n\), even the lower bound \(\Omega(1/s)\) "can be significantly larger than" the \(O(1/\sqrt n)\) of ordinary empirical risk minimisation (Remark 3.6). For scale: CIFAR-100 has \(n=45{,}000\) training images (Table 2), so \(\sqrt n\approx212\); the paper's batch sizes are 64 or 128 (Table 3).
Part 4 · Key idea · Sec. 4.1 · Eq. 6–7

Learn the quantile: quantile regression with the pinball loss

A quantile is the minimiser of an average loss. So it can be a variable trained by gradient updates, instead of something computed by sorting.

Eq. 6–7

The pinball loss and its minimiser (toy)

1One score: a tilted V

\[ \rho_\alpha(q,S)=\begin{cases}(1-\alpha)(S-q), & \text{if } S\ge q,\\ \alpha\,(q-S), & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}\qquad(6) \]

\(S\) is a real value and \(q\) the quantile prediction. A score above \(q\) costs \(1-\alpha\) per unit; a score below \(q\) costs only \(\alpha\). With \(\alpha=0.1\), leaving a score above \(q\) is nine times as expensive as leaving one below, so the minimiser pushes \(q\) up until only about a fraction \(\alpha\) of scores remain above it.

Toy score \(S=0.60\). The dot is the current \(q\).

2Average over scores: the minimiser is the \((1-\alpha)\)-quantile

\[ q^*\in\arg\min_q\ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\rho_\alpha(q,S_i)\qquad(7) \]

Between two neighbouring scores the average is a straight line. Its slope is the fraction of scores below \(q\), minus \(1-\alpha\):

\[ \begin{aligned}\frac{d}{dq}\,\frac1n\sum_{i}\rho_\alpha(q,S_i)&=\widehat F_n(q)-(1-\alpha),\\ \widehat F_n(q)&=\frac{\#\{i:\ S_i\lt q\}}{n}\end{aligned} \]

The slope turns positive at the \(\lceil n(1-\alpha)\rceil\)-th smallest score, so that is the minimiser. When \(n(1-\alpha)\) is a whole number the slope is zero between two scores and every \(q\) there is a minimiser; the paper notes that this solution set is "typically" not unique. With 12 toy scores that happens at \(\alpha=0.25\).

score below \(q\)score above \(q\)ring: minimisershort line: slope at \(q\)
The whole lower-level update
\[ \begin{aligned}\widehat\nabla_q\widehat{\mathcal L}^{\rm QR}&=\frac1{|\mathcal B|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal B}\Big[\alpha\,\one(S_i\lt q)-(1-\alpha)\,\one(S_i\gt q)\Big]\\ &=\widehat F_{\mathcal B}(q)-(1-\alpha)\end{aligned} \]
Differentiating Eq. 6 on a batch (no score exactly at \(q\)). The update \(q\leftarrow q-\gamma\,\widehat\nabla_q\) raises \(q\) when fewer than a fraction \(1-\alpha\) of the batch scores lie below it, and lowers it otherwise: by at most \(\gamma(1-\alpha)\) up and \(\gamma\alpha\) down. No sorting, just a count.
Which quantile The pinball minimiser is the \(\lceil n(1-\alpha)\rceil\)-th smallest training score. Split CP's \(\widehat Q_f\) uses rank \(\lceil(1-\alpha)(1+m)\rceil\) on calibration data, which carries the finite-sample correction. The learned \(q\) serves only during training; the test-time threshold is calibrated afresh (Part 9). Parameterising the quantile by a single variable \(q\) has been used in the CP literature before (Gibbs et al., 2023, and others the paper cites); the paper says DPSM is the first to use it for conformal training.
Part 5 · Method · Sec. 4.1 · Eq. 8–9

DPSM: set size on top, quantile regression underneath

The quantile becomes a variable \(q\). The lower level asks \(q\) to be the \((1-\alpha)\)-quantile of the true-label scores; the upper level trains \(f\) to make sets small at threshold \(q\).

Eq. 8–9

The bilevel problem

\[ \min_{f,q}\ \widehat{\mathcal L}_{\rm cls}(f)+\lambda\cdot\widehat{\mathcal L}^{\rm DM}_c(f,q)\qquad \text{s.t.}\ \ q\in\mathcal U(f)\triangleq\arg\min_{q'}\widehat{\mathcal L}^{\rm QR}(f,q')\qquad(8) \]

Upper level: classification and set size

\[ \widehat{\mathcal L}^{\rm DM}_c(f,q)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\Big[\sum_{y\in\mathcal Y}\tilde\one\big[S_f(X_i,y)\le q\big]\Big]\qquad(9) \]
The same soft set size as SA's \(\hat\ell\) in Eq. 5, except that the threshold is the variable \(q\) instead of a random batch quantile \(\hat q_f\sim\mathcal Q_f\). It uses every class's score.

Lower level: quantile regression

\[ \widehat{\mathcal L}^{\rm QR}(f,q)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\rho_\alpha\big(q,S_f(X_i,Y_i)\big) \]
The average pinball loss of the \(n\) true-label scores. By Part 4 its minimisers are their \((1-\alpha)\)-quantile; \(\mathcal U(f)\) is a set because the minimiser need not be unique.
\[ \text{implicit conformal loss:}\qquad \bar{\mathcal L}^{\rm DM}_c(f)\triangleq\min_{q\in\mathcal U(f)}\widehat{\mathcal L}^{\rm DM}_c(f,q) \]
  • The key change from SA is "to explicitly parameterize the quantile with a single score variable \(q\)". Conditioned on \(q\), "regardless of how accurate \(q\) is during training relative to the true \(Q_f\)", the set-size loss takes part in training.
  • The benefit: it "decouples the quantile \(q\) from the stochastic batches". \(q\) depends on \(f\) through the lower-level problem, not directly on whichever batch was drawn.
  • If an optimiser drives both variables to their solution sets, \(q\to\mathcal U(f)\) and \(f\to\arg\min_f\widehat{\mathcal L}_{\rm cls}(f)+\lambda\bar{\mathcal L}^{\rm DM}_c(f)\), the error in the conformal loss no longer depends on \(s\) (Part 7). Whether the paper's simple algorithm does this is the open question of Part 6.

The paper solves the bilevel problem with "a simple stochastic first-order algorithm" that updates \(f\) and \(q\) at the same time. It does not prove that this algorithm converges.

Sec. 4.2

Convergence: what is proved, and what is left open

Standard bilevel results do not cover this problem. \(\widehat{\mathcal L}_{\rm cls}\) and \(\widehat{\mathcal L}^{\rm DM}_c\) need not be convex in \(f\) and \(q\), and the lower-level pinball loss is "not continuously differentiable, not strongly convex, and typically has non-unique solution set". Implicit-gradient methods such as Ghadimi & Wang (2018) need a strongly convex lower level, so they cannot handle it. The paper therefore studies a penalty reformulation, the setting of Chen et al. (2024b):

\[ \min_{f,q}\ \widehat{\mathcal L}_{\rm cls}(f)+\lambda\cdot\widehat{\mathcal L}^{\rm DM}_c(f,q)+\sigma\big(\widehat{\mathcal L}^{\rm QR}(f,q)-\widehat{\mathcal L}^{\rm QR}(f,q^*)\big),\qquad q^*\in\mathcal U(f)\qquad(10) \]
Proved, about the problem Definition 4.3 (Hölderian error bound, HEB): \(h\) satisfies HEB if, for some \(\nu\ge1\) and \(c>0\), \(\mathrm{dist}(x,X^*)^\nu\le c\big(h(x)-\min_{x'\in X^*}h(x')\big)\) for all \(x\) in its domain, where \(X^*\) is its set of minimisers. Lemma 4.5: with no ties among the scores and \(f\) fixed, \(\widehat{\mathcal L}^{\rm QR}(f,q)\) satisfies HEB in \(q\) with \(\nu=1\) and some \(c>0\). (Intuition: the pinball average is convex and piecewise linear, so it grows at least linearly away from its minimisers.) Corollary 4.6: if \(\widehat{\mathcal L}_{\rm cls}\) and \(\widehat{\mathcal L}^{\rm DM}_c\) are \(L_{\rm cls}\)- and \(L_{\rm DM}\)-Lipschitz, then for any \(\epsilon>0\), with \(l=L_{\rm cls}+L_{\rm DM}\) and \(\sigma=cl\) in Eq. 10, an \(\epsilon\)-optimal solution of the penalised problem is an \((\epsilon,\epsilon/l)\)-optimal solution of the original problem (8), in the sense of Chen et al. (2024b), whose Theorem 2.7 it follows from.
Not proved, about Algorithm 1 That Algorithm 1 converges. In the paper's words, "it is non-trivial and an open challenge to develop a simple stochastic gradient optimization algorithm to solve a problem that shares similar conditions with DPSM. We leave this challenge for future work." Corollary 4.6 is about \(\epsilon\)-optimal solutions of the penalised problem (10). Algorithm 1 does not use the penalty, and nothing shows that it reaches such a solution. The support is empirical: Fig. 2 (next box and Part 8).
Checked empirically · Fig. 2 · CIFAR-100, DenseNet, HPS, 100 epochs The upper-level loss (classification plus set size) rises over the first 5 epochs, peaks, then decreases steadily and stabilises around epoch 35. The lower-level (pinball) loss drops sharply within the first 5 epochs, then decreases gradually until convergence near the end. The optimisation gaps of the conformal loss and of the pinball loss, comparing the learned quantile with the dataset-level quantile, both converge to nearly 0. The ResNet run (Fig. 6, App. F.1) behaves the same way, with the initial rise over the first 2 epochs. Sec. 4.2 credits the stable convergence of both levels to "employing standard practices for learning rates \(\eta\) and \(\gamma\)".
Part 7 · Theory · Thm. 3.5 · Thm. 4.1 · App. B–C · Fig. 4

The learning bound no longer depends on the batch size

For SA the error in the conformal loss is pinned between orders \(1/s\) and \(1/\sqrt s\). For DPSM's bilevel objective it is at most of order \(1/\sqrt n\), the usual rate of empirical risk minimisation.

SA · Theorem 3.5
\[ \Omega(1/s)\le\big|\widehat{\mathcal L}^{\rm SA}_c(f)-\mathcal L_c(f)\big|\le\tilde O(1/\sqrt s) \]
Assumptions 3.1 and 3.2; the rounding conditions of Part 3; \(s\le\sqrt n\); probability at least \(1-\delta\).
DPSM · Theorem 4.1
\[ \big|\bar{\mathcal L}^{\rm DM}_c(f)-\mathcal L_c(f)\big|\le\tilde O(1/\sqrt n) \]
Assumption 3.1 only; any classifier \(f\in\mathcal F\); probability at least \(1-\delta\). No \(s\) anywhere.
training-set size \(n\) (Table 2)

Shapes of the two bounds

With constants and log factors set to 1, only the slopes and the crossing mean anything. Theorem 3.5 applies for \(s\le\sqrt n\) (shaded); there, the SA lower bound \(1/s\) sits at or above DPSM's \(1/\sqrt n\).

Where the improvement comes from. The proof (App. C) restates the theorem at an exact lower-level solution, \(\big|\bar{\mathcal L}^{\rm DM}_c(f,q^*(f))-\mathcal L_c(f)\big|\le\tilde O(1/\sqrt n)\). It bounds the distance of that QR minimiser from the true quantile, \(|Q_f-q^*_f|\le\tilde O(1/\sqrt n)\) (Lemma C.2), and uses that the soft size is Lipschitz in \(q\) (Lemma C.3) plus a standard concentration bound (Lemma C.4). Remark 4.2: the gain is "mainly due to smaller error in estimating the true quantile, i.e., \(|q-Q_f|\)".

SA upper \(1/\sqrt s\) SA lower \(1/s\) DPSM \(1/\sqrt n\)
Read the comparison carefully Theorem 4.1 is about the bilevel objective with \(q\) an exact lower-level solution, \(q^*(f)\in\mathcal U(f)\). It says nothing about how close Algorithm 1's iterate \(q_t\) is to \(\mathcal U(f_t)\); that is the convergence question Part 6 leaves open, and Figs. 2–3 check it only empirically. And it compares an upper bound for DPSM with a lower bound for SA, each up to constants and log factors.
Assumptions

What the bounds assume, and how the paper checks it

Assumption 3.1 · bi-Lipschitz order statistics

\[ L_1\Big|\frac{j_1}{n}-\frac{j_2}{n}\Big|\le\big|S_{(j_1)}-S_{(j_2)}\big|\le L_2\Big|\frac{j_1}{n}-\frac{j_2}{n}\Big|,\quad L_1,L_2>0 \]
The sorted scores rise with normalised rank at a rate bounded below and above: no flat stretches, no jumps. Then a rank error turns into a score error of the same order. Both theorems use it. Fig. 4(a), CIFAR-100, DenseNet, HPS, calibration data: ConfTr's scores plotted against normalised order form a curve that stays away from both axes, which the paper reads as a slope bounded above and below.

Assumption 3.2 · local strong concavity

\[ \hat\ell(f,q)\ \text{is }\mu\text{-strongly concave in }q\ \text{locally around }\mathbb E[\hat q_f],\ \text{for fixed }f \]
Near the batch quantile's mean, the soft set size bends downward. SA's \(\Omega(1/s)\) lower bound rests on it (Lemmas B.3–B.4, Part 3). Only Theorem 3.5 uses it. Fig. 4(b), same setting: ConfTr's soft set size against coverage rate in \([0.02,0.98]\) in increments of 0.02, is concave near the target coverage 0.9 (zoomed-in panel).

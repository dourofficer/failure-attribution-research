How DCF trains a claim scorer through coherent factuality

Coherent factuality (CF) filters an LLM's reasoning with discrete steps: threshold the claims, drop any claim missing an ancestor, take a supremum, take a quantile. None of them passes a useful gradient, so CF's claim scorer is hand-made. DCF swaps each step for a smooth stand-in, trains a scorer \(\pi_\theta\) through them to keep more true claims, then puts the trained scorer back into discrete CF. The diagram follows the paper's Fig. 5 and Algorithms 1–3. Every number below it is a toy.

Part 1 · Sec. 3.5–3.6 · Alg. 3 · Fig. 5

One training update

Each epoch splits the training graphs into \(\mathcal D_{\rm cal}\) and \(\mathcal D_{\rm pred}\). Calibration turns \(\mathcal D_{\rm cal}\) into one threshold \(\hat\tau_\alpha\). Prediction turns \(\hat\tau_\alpha\) and each graph of \(\mathcal D_{\rm pred}\) into retention probabilities \(q_v\). The loss rewards true claims that are retained.

Part 2 · Sec. 3.2–3.3 · Eq. 1–6 · Alg. 1

Differentiable calibration: from one graph to \(\hat\tau_\alpha\)

Each calibration graph is reduced to one number, the largest threshold that keeps none of its false claims. A quantile of those numbers is the threshold.

Temperatures \(\alpha\)

These controls apply to Parts 2 and 3. Toy risks lie in [0, 1] with \(C=1\) and margin \(m=0.3\), so the temperatures here are not on the scale of the paper's grid-searched values (App. D.3). In every figure, a dark outline or ring marks what discrete CF does with the same risks.

1Score every claim, build the grid

The scorer gives each claim a confidence \(\pi_\theta(x_v)\). Its risk is \(r_v=C-\pi_\theta(x_v)\). The candidate thresholds are the risks themselves, plus one margin point at each end.

\[ \begin{aligned}\mathcal T&=\{\tau_{\min},\,r_{v_1},\dots,r_{v_n},\,\tau_{\max}\}\\ \tau_{\min}&=\min_v r_v-m,\quad \tau_{\max}=\max_v r_v+m\end{aligned} \]

Claims c3 and c5 are false. c5 has a low risk of its own, but it is built on c3.

2Soft keep, then ancestor coherence

CF keeps claim \(v\) at threshold \(\tau\) if \(r_v\le\tau\) and every ancestor is kept too. DCF turns the first test into a sigmoid and the "and" into a weighted geometric mean over the claim and its ancestors.

\[ p_{v,\tau}=\sigma\Big(\frac{\tau-r_v}{T_p}\Big) \]
\[ \log q_{v,\tau}=\frac{\sum_{u\in\mathrm{Anc}(v)\cup\{v\}}w_u\log p_{u,\tau}}{\sum_{u\in\mathrm{Anc}(v)\cup\{v\}}w_u} \]\(w_u=\gamma\) for an ancestor, \(w_v=1\) for the claim itself.

3Violation: do the false claims get through?

For each threshold, \(Q_\tau\) is the geometric mean, over the false claims \(V^-\), of the probability that each one is filtered out. The violation \(V_\tau\) is near 0 when they are all gone and near 1 when one gets through.

\[ \begin{aligned}\log Q_\tau&=\frac{1}{|V^-|}\sum_{v\in V^-}\log(1-q_{v,\tau})\\ V_\tau&=1-Q_\tau^{1/\tau_s}\end{aligned} \]

This step needs the labels \(y_v\), so it exists only in calibration. In the toy, a graph with no false claim gets \(V_\tau=0\) at every threshold. Lower \(\tau_s\) while \(T_p\) is still large and every threshold looks like a violation: Theorem 3.1 sends \(T_p\to0\) first for that reason.

\(V_\tau\)discrete CF: the subgraph \(U_\tau\) holds a false claim

4Soft supremum: one score per graph

CF's nonconformity score is the largest threshold whose subgraph, and every smaller one, holds no false claim. DCF gives each threshold a utility, its size minus its violation, and takes a softmax-weighted average of the thresholds.

\[ \begin{aligned}s_\tau&=\lambda\,\tau-V_\tau\\ w^{\rm cal}_\tau&=\frac{e^{\beta s_\tau}}{\sum_{\tau'\in\mathcal T}e^{\beta s_{\tau'}}}\\ \tilde\tau&=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal T}w^{\rm cal}_\tau\,\tau\end{aligned} \]

Both \(\tau\) and \(V_\tau\) are min-max scaled to [0, 1] before the utility is computed (the paper's footnote 5), and the toy fixes \(\lambda=1\). Appendix Alg. 1 writes the utility as \(\tau-\lambda V_\tau\); the two forms agree at \(\lambda=1\).

5Soft quantile over the calibration set

Moves 1–4 run on every calibration graph. The threshold has to be one that at least a \(1-\alpha\) share of them tolerate, so it is the \(k\)-th largest score with \(k=\lceil(1-\alpha)(n+1)\rceil\). DCF reads that position from a soft sort.

\[ \begin{aligned}\hat\tau_\alpha&=\mathrm{SoftQuantile}\big(\{\tilde\tau_i\}_{i=1}^n,\,q,\,\rho\big)\\ q&=\lceil(1-\alpha)(n+1)\rceil/n\end{aligned} \]

The paper names the operator (Grover et al., 2019) and its sharpness \(\rho\) but gives no formula, so the toy uses that paper's NeuralSort relaxation with temperature \(1/\rho\). Counting from the largest score is this page's reading of the quantile. It is the direction in which the coverage guarantee holds, and it fits the paper's remark that thresholds saturate at \(\tau_{\max}\) once \(\alpha\) exceeds the share of graphs with errors.

Differentiable prediction and the retention loss

The threshold from Part 2 filters each prediction graph. The result is one retention probability per claim, and the loss counts how many true claims survive.

Toy

One prediction graph, filtered at \(\hat\tau_\alpha\)

6Gated soft argmax

Moves 1–2 run again on the prediction graph, on its own grid. CF then picks the largest threshold below \(\hat\tau_\alpha\). DCF weights every threshold by \(e^{\beta\tau}\), which favours large ones, times a sigmoid gate that closes above \(\hat\tau_\alpha\).

\[ w_\tau\ \propto\ e^{\beta\tau}\cdot\sigma\Big(\frac{\hat\tau_\alpha-\tau}{\tau_z}\Big) \]

\(\beta\) is the same one as in move 4. Read off the formula: above \(\hat\tau_\alpha\) the gate decays like \(e^{-\tau/\tau_z}\) while the first factor grows like \(e^{\beta\tau}\). So the gate can only win when \(\beta\,\tau_z<1\), and it needs to be well below 1. Raise \(\tau_z\) and watch the weight escape to \(\tau_{\max}\).

7Retention probabilities and the loss

Each claim's retention probability averages its membership \(q_{v,\tau}\) over the thresholds, with those weights. The loss is minus the number of true claims retained, averaged over the prediction graphs.

\[ \begin{aligned}q_v&=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal T}w_\tau\,q_{v,\tau}\\ \mathcal L_{\rm retention}&=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal D_{\rm pred}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal D_{\rm pred}}\sum_{v\in V_i}y_{i,v}\,q_{i,v}\end{aligned} \]

The claim whose risk equals the selected threshold has \(p=0.5\) at every temperature under Eq. 1, so its \(q\) stays below 1. Theorem 3.1 shifts the sigmoid by \(\sqrt{T_p}\) to remove this, and the paper notes that in practice a margin of 0 gives similar probabilities. Press Near the limit to see the soft and discrete outputs coincide.

After training The smooth pipeline is thrown away. The trained \(\pi_\theta\) supplies the risks, and discrete CF is calibrated and applied as usual, so the coverage guarantee is the one CF already had (Sec. 3.6). Theorem 3.1 says the smooth score \(\tilde\tau\) converges to CF's score when the limits are taken in order: \(T_p\to0^+\) first, then \(\tau_s\to0\), then \(\beta\to\infty\). Theorem 3.2 states the matching result for prediction, with \(T_p\to0^+\), \(\tau_z\to0^+\) and \(\beta\to\infty\).
